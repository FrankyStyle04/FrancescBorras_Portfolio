Chrono Fish

2023 - 2024

Chrono Fish is a pixel-art top-down roguelike where the protagonist can only dodge and parry.

I worked as Game and Level Designer, defining the metrics behind the procedural temple and tuning the core parry timing.

Task Overview

  • Defined level metrics driving coherent procedural map generation.
  • Tuned the parry window against enemy attack timings until failure felt fair.
  • Designed room threat density so every space is beatable without attacking.
  • Balanced the scoring system to reward cleaner, less frequent parries.

Context

Chrono Fish is a pixel-art top-down roguelike with an unusual design constraint: the protagonist is a fish who can't attack, only dodge and parry. The demo is set inside an Aztec temple whose final boss is Kukulkan, the feathered serpent. I worked as Game & Level Designer in a team of 4.

The problem: designing without the simplest tool

Removing the direct attack forces all the challenge to live in timing and enemy reading. Without that tool, a poorly calibrated room feels unfair immediately: the player has no way to "force" a situation through raw damage, so every room has to be honestly designed to be beatable with dodge and parry alone.

To support that I defined coherent level metrics (enemy approach distances, parry windows, threat density per room) which I then used to generate the temple's procedural maps, instead of leaving procedural generation to purely aesthetic criteria.

The game itself rewards parrying less: the cleaner the parry, the higher the score. The mechanic and the scoring system tell the same story about mastery.

Tuning the parry window

Parry is the mechanic the whole game rests on, so its timing window was iterated more than any other system. Too generous a margin makes the challenge trivial; too tight turns every death into frustration instead of a lesson. I tuned the timing alongside enemy attack windows until failing a parry felt like a late decision by the player, not an imposition by the system.

What I learned

Designing around a strong constraint demands a discipline that a wide mechanics kit doesn't require: every room has to justify why it's beatable with only the tools the player already has. It was my first real procedural generation project, and I learned that badly defined level metrics show up much faster in a procedural system than in a hand-crafted one, because the mistakes repeat in every generated map.
