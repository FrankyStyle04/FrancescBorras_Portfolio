Denshattack!

RoleQA & Game Design Assistant
StudioUndercoders
Duration2025-2026
PublisherFireshine Games

Overview

Denshattack! is an arcade train-flipping platformer: you flip, trick and grind a customisable train through a colourful Japanese dystopia, outmatching rival gangs and a shady megacorp across more than 60 levels and 8 regions. It shipped on PC, Xbox and PlayStation, published by Fireshine Games. This was my internship at Undercoders, working as a bridge between design, programming and QA.

My contributions

My job had to survive the jump from documentation to real implementation, not just read well in a PDF. That meant staying close to both the design intent and the day-to-day production reality of a live project.

Progression & economy balance: I balanced progression and economy across 67 handcrafted levels, tuning the unlock pace in Excel and Google Sheets so difficulty and rewards escalated at a rate that felt earned rather than grindy.

Documentation: I authored 6 comprehensive Game Design Documents to communicate gameplay systems, progression logic and implementation requirements across a multidisciplinary team, and kept that documentation current in Confluence to support cross-team collaboration.

Gameplay & QA: I helped design and refine gameplay features by working closely with designers, programmers and QA, keeping player experience aligned with project goals, and validated level flow and difficulty balance through structured internal playtesting, translating feedback into concrete design changes.

Working at the intersection of design and QA meant every balance change I proposed had to be re-tested by hand before it could ship, a discipline that shaped how I document changes for other projects too.

Puzzle-Adventure / Unreal Engine 5

Lyra

RoleGame & Level Designer
TeamLa Mapachanda, 7 people
Duration2025-2026
RecognitionBest Thesis, ENTI DemoDay 2026

Context

Lyra is a 3D wholesome puzzle-adventure in which you accompany Pochi (fire dragon) and Mochi (water dragon) through sunlit hills and glowing crystal caves. It's my thesis project at ENTI-UB, built with a team of 7 people under the name La Mapachanda. My role covered mechanics design, level design, and validating both with real playtest data.

The problem: document before you build

With two dragons that have distinct abilities (fire to melt and ignite, water to grow bridges and fill wells, and both usable as projectiles to reach distant areas) the real risk was combinatorial: if every mechanic is designed in isolation, the player doesn't learn a system, they learn a list of unrelated tricks.

Before touching the level editor, I built a Pattern-Based Dictionary: a catalog of atomic mechanics (fire shot, water shot, dragon throw) and the patterns that emerge when they're combined. Every room in the game wasn't designed as "a puzzle idea", it was designed as the specific combination of patterns I wanted to teach at that point.

Each pattern in the dictionary is tagged with the EDPV stage in which it's introduced, demonstrated, practiced and validated.

Level structure: the EDPV curve

On top of that dictionary I applied the EDPV model (Exposure, Demonstration, Practice, Validation) to the game's three thematic zones, ordering difficulty and emotional pacing deliberately instead of leaving it to intuition. A new mechanic is first seen, then demonstrated in a risk-free context, then practiced with rising complexity, and finally validated in a puzzle that requires combining it with everything learned before.

The Puzzle 6 problem: when design isn't enough

During internal playtesting, one of the puzzles in the crystal cave (Puzzle 6) generated more navigation errors than expected. My hypothesis as a designer was that the visual guidance, the cues that tell the player where to look, was insufficient. Instead of redesigning it on instinct, I decided to test it.

I instrumented telemetry in C++ on Unreal Engine 5, capturing player position and actions as structured CSV, and designed a real A/B test: stratified random assignment, inclusion/exclusion criteria, a moderator script and informed consent. I compared the original layout against a redesign with clearer visual guidance, and analyzed the results in Python with Shapiro-Wilk, Student's t-test, and Cohen's d.

The p-value wasn't statistically significant. The effect size (Cohen's d ~1.0) was large. I reported both, not just the one that supported my hypothesis.

That honesty in reporting is, to me, as much a part of the design as the puzzle redesign itself: an uncomfortable data point, well documented, is worth more than a nice conclusion without evidence.

Result

The visual guidance redesign of Puzzle 6 reduced navigation errors by 35% and improved resolution time by 33% compared to the original layout, measured with the same telemetry pipeline. Lyra won Best Thesis at ENTI DemoDay 2026.

What I learned

The most valuable part wasn't confirming my design hypothesis, it was learning to separate what "feels" better from what's demonstrably better. A non-significant p-value alongside a large effect size is a real grey area in user research, and I learned how to communicate it to a design team without inflating it or dismissing it.

Local Party Game / Unity C#

Tumble Up

RoleProducer & Game Designer
TeamTxispa's Studio, 5 people
Duration2024-2025
Players2v2 local

Context

Tumble Up is a local 2v2 party game where two pairs compete across physical minigames: one moment you're high-fiving your partner, the next you're pushing them off the edge to win solo. I designed 4 minigames and 8 maps within a team of 5 people, and also served as SCRUM master for the project.

The problem: matches that don't hold themselves together

A party game lives or dies on the rhythm between matches, not within a single match. If the loop between minigames is slow, the group drifts apart; if it's too short, there's no time to register who's winning or for the rivalry and inside jokes that make a session memorable.

I designed the match loops of 2 to 7 minigames as the real unit of design, not the individual minigame. Every map was built to enter and leave rotation without breaking the group's rhythm, and the variety across 4 distinct mechanics exists so the same group can extend a session without it feeling repetitive.

Character customization (cat, dog, monkey) doubles as a social tool: pairs can share a skin to reinforce "we're a team" right before the minigame turns them against each other.

The other role: holding the team together, not just the game

As SCRUM master I ran the whole project's organization: tracking spreadsheets, dailies, and deciding which minigames entered each sprint. I learned that a good match loop starts with a good team loop: if the team doesn't deliver on time, the match-loop playtesting never gets validated.

What I learned

Designing for "fun in a group" is different from designing for a solo player: success isn't just whether the minigame works, it's whether the whole session leaves people wanting one more round. I also learned the limits of carrying design and production at once: in the heaviest SCRUM weeks, design iteration time was what suffered most, a tension I want to manage better on the next project.

Top-Down Roguelike / Unity C#

Chrono Fish

RoleGame & Level Designer
TeamOlympia Interactive, 4 people
Duration2023-2024
Core mechanicParry, no direct attack

Context

Chrono Fish is a pixel-art top-down roguelike with an unusual design constraint: the protagonist is a fish who can't attack, only dodge and parry. The demo is set inside an Aztec temple whose final boss is Kukulkan, the feathered serpent. I worked as Game & Level Designer in a team of 4.

The problem: designing without the simplest tool

Removing the direct attack forces all the challenge to live in timing and enemy reading. Without that tool, a poorly calibrated room feels unfair immediately: the player has no way to "force" a situation through raw damage, so every room has to be honestly designed to be beatable with dodge and parry alone.

To support that I defined coherent level metrics (enemy approach distances, parry windows, threat density per room) which I then used to generate the temple's procedural maps, instead of leaving procedural generation to purely aesthetic criteria.

The game itself rewards parrying less: the cleaner the parry, the higher the score. The mechanic and the scoring system tell the same story about mastery.

Tuning the parry window

Parry is the mechanic the whole game rests on, so its timing window was iterated more than any other system. Too generous a margin makes the challenge trivial; too tight turns every death into frustration instead of a lesson. I tuned the timing alongside enemy attack windows until failing a parry felt like a late decision by the player, not an imposition by the system.

0playable temple with a final boss

What I learned

Designing around a strong constraint demands a discipline that a wide mechanics kit doesn't require: every room has to justify why it's beatable with only the tools the player already has. It was my first real procedural generation project, and I learned that badly defined level metrics show up much faster in a procedural system than in a hand-crafted one, because the mistakes repeat in every generated map.
